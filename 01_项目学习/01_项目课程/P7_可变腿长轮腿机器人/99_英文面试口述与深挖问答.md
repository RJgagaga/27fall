## 1. 三个版本口述

### 30 秒

> I worked on a lab wheel-legged robot whose leg length can change. My part was balance control and on-robot tuning. I built a gain-scheduled LQR on body pitch and motion states, where the gains change with the leg length, and a virtual model controller that maps support force and pitch torque to the joint motors through the Jacobian. I deployed it on an STM32H7 with FreeRTOS and FDCAN, and tuned it on the real robot, so it kept balance and tracked speed commands while the legs extended and retracted.

要点：腿长可变 → 增益调度 LQR → VMC → STM32H7 实车。

### 90 秒

> The robot has two wheels and two five-bar legs, so it is an unstable system that must be actively balanced, and its dynamics change with the leg length. I was responsible for the balance algorithm and the tuning.
>
> First, state and model. I computed the virtual leg length and angle from the closed-chain kinematics, combined the leg angle with the IMU pitch, and estimated the body velocity from wheel speed, leg motion and acceleration. That gives six states: leg angle and rate, position and velocity error, body pitch and rate.
>
> Second, control. I designed LQR gains offline on a grid of leg lengths and scheduled them with polynomials in the leg length. The LQR outputs a wheel-hub torque and a hip torque. A leg-length PID with gravity feedforward gives the support force, and VMC maps the support force and the hip torque to the two joint torques with the Jacobian transpose.
>
> Third, deployment. On the STM32H7 with FreeRTOS and FDCAN I tuned the robot, and when balance failed I traced it to state inconsistency, torque limits or ground-contact transitions, and fixed each one.

### 3 分钟

> (Start with the 90-second version, then add the following three blocks.)
>
> **Why gain scheduling.** When the leg length changes, the center of mass height, the inertia and the torque lever arms change, so the linearized A and B matrices change. In my model the unstable pole scales roughly as one over the square root of the length, and the input gain changes several times over the range. A fixed gain is only good near its design length, so I solved the Riccati equation at several lengths and fitted each gain element with a polynomial, then clamped the leg length to the fitted range online.
>
> **Why VMC.** The legs must be compliant and force-controlled, because the balance controller produces torques, not positions. VMC lets me define a virtual spring and damper along the leg, add gravity feedforward, and convert the desired force to joint torques by the Jacobian transpose, which follows from virtual work. I also handled roll with opposite force corrections on the two legs, so total support stays the same.
>
> **Debugging.** The hardest part was not the equations but the real robot. Sign conventions and zero offsets gave inconsistent states; torque limits made the controller lose its linear region; and ground-contact transitions switched the dynamics. I added checks, rate limits and mode logic for each. The result is that the robot balanced and followed velocity commands during leg-length changes.
>
> A concrete example: at first the robot crept forward slowly even when I held it still. I hung it up and read the pitch, and it showed a constant offset of about one and a half degrees, because the center of mass sits a bit in front of the wheel axis and the IMU is not mounted perfectly level. I calibrated that equilibrium offset into the pitch state, and after that the robot stood within about three centimeters over thirty seconds.

## 2. STAR 案例（用你真实经历替换细节）

### 案例 A：状态不一致（State inconsistency）

- **S**：Robot balance was unstable on the real hardware although the controller was stable in the model.
- **T**：Find out why and make the states trustworthy.
- **A**：I checked each state against a hand test: tilt the body, move one joint, push the wheel. I found that a sign or zero offset in some state (for example the pitch or the leg angle) did not match the model convention, and that the IMU and the joint feedback were sampled at different times. I unified the conventions, calibrated the zero offsets, and used a consistent snapshot per cycle.
- **R**：The estimated states matched the model and the balance became consistent.The quantity that was wrong was the body pitch: it had a constant offset of about 1.5 degrees (0.026 rad), and the IMU and joint data also came from slightly different instants, so I took one snapshot per cycle. After calibration the robot stood still within about 3 cm over 30 seconds.（具体量：俯仰零位偏置约 1.5°，另有 IMU 与关节数据不同步，改为每周期一次快照。）

### 案例 B：力矩限幅（Torque limits）

- **S**：Under larger disturbances or speed commands the robot suddenly lost balance.
- **T**：Understand the role of saturation.
- **A**：I logged the outputs and saw the hip or wheel torque hitting its limit; the effective gain dropped and the loop left its linear region. I ramped the velocity command, limited the leg-length rate, retuned Q and R to reduce torque demand, and added anti-windup.
- **R**：The robot tolerated a 0.5 m/s speed command without losing balance: the wheel torque demand, which would have been around 9 N·m for a step against a 3 N·m limit, dropped to well inside the limit with a one-second ramp.（中文：0.5 m/s 阶跃理论需求约 9 N·m，限幅 3 N·m；改 1 s 斜坡后需求远低于限幅。）

### 案例 C：离地切换（Ground-contact transition）

- **S**：The robot misbehaved when a wheel lost contact or when it landed.
- **T**：Detect contact and switch control safely.
- **A**：I estimated the support force on the wheel, used a threshold with debouncing, set the wheel torque and position feedback to a safe mode in the air, and reset the integrators and the position on landing.
- **R**：Contact transitions no longer caused torque spikes: I used a force threshold of 10 N to leave and 20 N to return, with 16 ms and 10 ms debouncing, and ramped the controller back in over about 80 ms after landing.（中文：离地阈值 10 N、着地 20 N，去抖 16 ms / 10 ms，落地后约 80 ms 渐入。）

（以上三案例对应 CV 的三类异常；案例中的数值是本项目的取值，与 README 自查表一致。）

## 3. 问答

### A. 总览

**1. What is a wheel-legged robot and why not a wheeled or legged one?**
> It combines wheel speed and efficiency with leg height adjustment and terrain adaptation. The cost is active balance and variable dynamics.
追问：*What are its weaknesses?* — 控制复杂，接触切换，能耗在站立时。

**2. Describe your control architecture.**
> Gain-scheduled LQR for balance, leg-length PID plus feedforward for support force, VMC to joint torques, FDCAN to the motors.

**3. What was your personal contribution?**
> Balance algorithms and on-robot tuning: the state construction, the scheduled LQR, the VMC, and the debugging. The mechanical platform was provided by the lab.

**4. What are the inputs and outputs of your LQR?**
> Six states in, two torques out: wheel-hub torque and hip torque.

**5. What did you achieve?**
> The robot kept its body balanced and tracked velocity commands while the leg length changed.
追问：*Numbers?* — 见第 7 节模板。

### B. 运动学

**6. How do you compute the foot position from the motor angles?**
> The motor angles give two joint points directly; the foot is the intersection of two circles around them with radii equal to the passive link lengths.

**7. Why two solutions, and how do you choose?**
> Two circles intersect twice, giving two assembly modes. I pick the one that matches the mounting, the wheel below the hip line, and for our dimensions the two modes never merge physically.

**8. How do you do inverse kinematics?**
> Two independent circle intersections: each active joint lies on the circle around the hip with the active link length and the circle around the foot with the passive link length. Four candidates, one chosen by angle range.

**9. What is the Jacobian here and how do you derive it?**
> It maps joint rates to foot velocity. Each passive link has constant length, so the velocity components along the link at both ends are equal. That gives two linear equations, which I solve for the foot velocity.

**10. What is a singularity of the five-bar?**
> Where the Jacobian loses rank: the passive links collinear, or an active link collinear with its passive link at full extension. I keep the leg length away from it.

**11. What is the virtual leg?**
> The line from the hip midpoint to the wheel axle. Its length and angle reparametrize the two degrees of freedom.

**12. How do you compute leg length rate and angle rate?**
> Project the foot velocity on the leg direction for the length rate, and take the perpendicular part divided by the length for the angle rate, via the Jacobian.

**13. How do you get the leg angle used in control?**
> World-frame leg angle: IMU pitch plus the leg angle relative to the body, measured from the vertical. The sign convention is checked on the robot.

### C. 建模与 LQR

**14. How did you model the robot?**
> A sagittal-plane three-body inverted pendulum: wheel, leg, body. Lagrange equations, motor torques as generalized forces by virtual work, linearized around upright.

**15. Why is the model linear and when does it break?**
> Small-angle linearization; it breaks for large tilt, slip, saturation, and fast leg-length changes.

**16. What does Q and R mean?**
> Q penalizes state error, R penalizes control effort; only their ratio matters.

**17. How did you choose Q and R?**
> Start with Bryson's rule, one over the square of the acceptable value, then adjust on the robot watching torque saturation and oscillation. My diagonal Q ran from 1 to 5000 with the pitch weight largest, and R was 1 for the wheel and 0.25 for the hip.（本项目：Q 对角 1–5000，俯仰最大；R = diag(1, 0.25)。）

**18. What happens if you increase the pitch weight?**
> The body becomes stiffer, but the hip torque demand rises roughly in proportion.

**19. Is LQR robust?**
> For continuous-time LQR with full state feedback it has good guaranteed margins in theory, but delay, saturation and model error erode them, which is why I tuned on hardware.

**20. Why is the sign of the velocity gain counterintuitive?**
> Non-minimum phase: to slow down you must first accelerate the wheel so that the body leans back.

**21. How do you handle tracking a velocity command with LQR?**
> Use the error to the reference in the state, integrate the velocity command into a position reference, and ramp the command.

**22. Why not integral action?**
> LQR has none by itself; I rely on the position and velocity terms and feedforward, and could augment the state if needed. I did not add an integrator; the small static offsets were handled by the equilibrium calibration and a bounded position term.（本项目未加积分。）

**23. Continuous or discrete design?**
> Continuous design is fine when the sample time is much smaller than the fastest closed-loop time constant; otherwise use discrete LQR on the ZOH model.

### D. 增益调度

**24. Why schedule by leg length?**
> The plant depends on it: mass matrix and gravity term change, the unstable pole scales as one over the square root of length, and the input gain changes several times.

**25. How did you build K(L)?**
> Riccati at a grid of lengths, fit each element with a polynomial, check stability on a dense grid, clamp the length online.

**26. How many coefficients?**
> Twelve elements; with cubic fits that is 48 numbers. I used cubic fits over a leg length of 0.10 to 0.30 meters.（本项目：三次，区间 0.10–0.30 m。）

**27. Why polynomials instead of a lookup table?**
> Smooth, small, fixed compute; tables need interpolation and have derivative jumps at nodes. Both are valid.

**28. What if the leg length leaves the fitted range?**
> Extrapolation error grows, so I clamp it. In my teaching example one gain at 0.5 m was off by almost half.

**29. Is gain scheduling guaranteed stable?**
> Not in general, because the frozen-time analysis ignores the rate of change. So I limit the leg-length rate and validate on the robot.

**30. What would you do for a better scheme?**
> LPV or robust design, or MPC with the leg length as a measured parameter.

### E. VMC

**31. What is VMC?**
> Define desired force by a virtual spring-damper and map it to joint torques with the Jacobian transpose.

**32. Why the transpose?**
> Power balance: tau transpose times joint rate equals force transpose times foot velocity for all rates.

**33. How do you map support force and hip torque to joints?**
> Generalized forces on the leg length and angle; compose the foot Jacobian with the polar map and transpose.

**34. Why is VMC better than position control here?**
> It matches the force outputs of the balance loop and gives compliance against ground impact.

**35. What happens in the symmetric pose?**
> The support force becomes a differential joint torque and the hip torque a common-mode one.

**36. Numerical sense of the torques?**
> Teaching estimate: about 0.1 m/rad Jacobian entry and 30 N support gives about 3 N·m per hip joint. On our robot the software limit was 6 N·m per joint, so standing used about half of it.（本项目关节限幅 6 N·m。）

**37. What is the feedforward in the support force?**
> About half the body weight divided by the cosine of the leg angle.

**38. How do you handle roll?**
> Add support force to one leg and subtract from the other, so total support is unchanged.

**39. What prevents the legs from splitting?**
> A differential PD on the leg angles added to the hip torques, since LQR only sees the average.

**40. Units trap?**
> mm versus m in the Jacobian; I write units in the code and check a static case.

### F. 估计与接触

**41. How do you estimate velocity?**
> Absolute wheel rate plus leg motion terms, fused with IMU acceleration in a Kalman filter.

**42. Why fuse?**
> Wheel velocity has no drift but fails on slip; integrated acceleration is smooth but drifts.

**43. How to handle slip?**
> Monitor the innovation, inflate the measurement noise, limit torque.

**44. How to remove gravity from the accelerometer?**
> Use the pitch estimate: horizontal acceleration is about the axis reading plus g times pitch.

**45. How do you detect that a wheel left the ground?**
> Estimate the ground force from the support force, hip torque, wheel weight and vertical acceleration; below a threshold with debouncing means airborne.

**46. What do you do in the air?**
> Zero the wheel torque and the position and velocity feedback, keep the leg length and posture under control, and limit the support force.

**47. What on landing?**
> Reset position and integrators, ramp the gains back, and avoid torque steps.

**48. Why debounce?**
> Bumps momentarily reduce the force and would cause false switches.

### G. 嵌入式

**49. Describe the software structure.**
> FreeRTOS tasks: IMU, control at a fixed period, CAN receive via interrupt, communication and safety monitor. In my setup the IMU sampled at 1 kHz, the control task ran at 500 Hz with a fixed period, and the monitor ran at about 100 Hz.（本项目：IMU 1 kHz，控制 500 Hz，监控约 100 Hz。）

**50. How do you share data between tasks?**
> Atomic snapshots or mutex-protected structures, timestamps for freshness.

**51. What is the FDCAN frame for the motors? (L1)**
> Typically an 8-byte frame with 16-bit position and 12-bit velocity, Kp, Kd and torque fields scaled between limits. Ours used the position range plus or minus 12.5 radians, velocity plus or minus 30, and torque plus or minus 10 newton-meters.
（中文：典型 8 字节经典帧，位置 16 位、速度 12 位，Kp、Kd、力矩按上下限量化；本项目编码范围如上。注意：FDCAN 是 STM32H7 外设名，这里用的是经典 CAN 模式，无 BRS。）

**52. Why quantization matters?**
> A 12-bit torque over ±10 N·m has a resolution of about 0.005 N·m, and the command is clamped to the range.

**53. Bus load?**
> The FDCAN peripheral runs in classic CAN mode, not CAN FD, with 8-byte frames at 1 Mbps. A classic frame is about 111 bits, up to about 135 with worst-case bit stuffing, so one frame takes 0.11 to 0.135 ms. The joint bus carries four command frames and four replies, eight frames per 2 ms cycle, which is 0.89 to 1.08 ms, or 44 to 54 percent. The wheel bus carries four frames, about 22 to 27 percent. That is why I split joints and wheels over two buses. For a faster loop I would cut the frame count or lower the rate, not change the frame format.
（中文：FDCAN 外设工作在经典 CAN 模式，8 字节帧、1 Mbps，不是 CAN FD。一帧约 111 位，最坏位填充约 135 位，即 0.11–0.135 ms。关节总线每 2 ms 周期 8 帧（4 命令 + 4 回复）：0.89–1.08 ms，占 44%–54%；轮总线 4 帧约 22%–27%。所以拆两路；想更快就减帧数或降频，而不是换帧格式。）

**54. What if CAN feedback stops?**
> Detect stale data by counters; command zero torque or a safe mode.

**55. How do you choose the control period?**
> Fast enough versus the closed-loop poles and delays, limited by the bus and the compute. I used 500 Hz, which is far above the closed-loop bandwidth of a few hertz and leaves about half of the bus free.（本项目 500 Hz。）

### H. 调试

**56. Name three classes of failure you found.**
> State inconsistency, torque limits, ground-contact transitions.

**57. First thing you check when it falls?**
> Signs and zero offsets with hand tests, then log states and torques.

**58. How do you know it is saturation?**
> Torque output sits at the limit while the state diverges; gains effectively drop.

**59. How do you tune safely?**
> Lift the robot, start with the leg loop, then low gains on the ground with a catch.

**60. What would you improve?**
> Better contact estimation, an adaptive or robust scheme, and systematic parameter identification.

### I. 个人

**61. What was hardest?**
> Making the real states agree with the model; the math was clean, the signs and timing were not.

**62. What did you learn?**
> Model, estimate, then control: most failures were in the states and limits, not in the gains.

**63. How is it related to your other projects?**
> P1 is the same inverted-pendulum idea on a ballbot; P3 and P2 share the embedded CAN execution chain.

## 4. LQR 数学深挖（L1 基本 / L2 追问 / L3 深挖）

**L1. What does the LQR minimize, and what do Q and R mean physically?**
> It minimizes the infinite-horizon cost J = ∫(sᵀQs + uᵀRu)dt. Q says how much I dislike each state error, R says how much I dislike each unit of torque. In my robot the largest Q is on body pitch, because pitch is what makes the robot fall. R is small on the wheel torque and larger on the hip torque, since the hip torque also disturbs the support force.
（中文：LQR 最小化无限时域二次代价。Q 表示对各状态误差的厌恶程度，R 表示对力矩的代价。本项目俯仰权重最大，因为它决定倒不倒；轮矩 R 小、髋矩 R 大。）

**L1. What are your Q and R?**
> Q = diag(100, 1, 500, 100, 5000, 1) and R = diag(1, 0.25) in my design: the leg angle, leg-angle rate, position, velocity, pitch and pitch rate. Pitch dominates. On the robot I only fine-tuned the pitch weight and the velocity terms. These are my design values and I treat them as a starting point, not as optimal numbers.
（中文：Q=diag(100,1,500,100,5000,1)，R=diag(1,0.25)；实车只微调俯仰与速度项；这是设计取值而非最优值。）

**L2. Derive the Riccati equation.**
> Assume the value function V = sᵀPs. The Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman condition says that minimizing sᵀQs + uᵀRu + 2sᵀP(As+Bu) over u gives zero derivative: 2Ru + 2BᵀPs = 0, so u = −R⁻¹BᵀPs. Substituting back gives AᵀP + PA − PBR⁻¹BᵀP + Q = 0. Then dV/dt = −(sᵀQs + uᵀRu) < 0, so V is a Lyapunov function and the closed loop is stable.
（中文：设 V=sᵀPs，对 u 求导为零得 u=−R⁻¹BᵀPs，代回得代数 Riccati 方程；V 的导数为负，是 Lyapunov 函数，闭环稳定。）

**L2. Under what conditions does a solution exist?**
> (A,B) must be stabilizable and (Q^½, A) detectable; then there is a unique positive semidefinite solution and the closed loop is stable. For my six-state model the controllability matrix has rank 6 over 0.1 to 0.3 m in the teaching example, so this holds. I check it numerically at every design length.
（中文：需要 (A,B) 可稳定、(Q^½,A) 可检测；教学模型在 0.1–0.3 m 内可控矩阵秩为 6；每个设计腿长都数值检查。）

**L2. Scalar and double-integrator examples (teaching examples).**
> For a=1, b=1, q=1, r=1 the Riccati equation gives p = 1+√2, K = 2.414, and the closed-loop pole is −1.414. With r = 0.1 the gain rises to 4.317 and the pole moves to −3.317: cheaper control means faster response. For the double integrator with Q = I and R = 1, K = [1, √3] and the poles are −0.866 ± 0.5j.
（中文：标量例与双积分器例，说明 R 变小则增益变大、极点更快。教学示例。）

**L2. Why six states and two outputs, when the course code has four states and one output?**
> The course code uses four states and only wheel torque, with the position gain set to zero. In my design the hip torque is a second input, so the controller can regulate the leg angle and body pitch separately, and position and velocity stay in the state so the robot can track a speed command. Six states and two inputs make K a 2×6 matrix: each row is one input, and both inputs see all six states.
（中文：课程代码四状态单输出；本项目六状态双输出，K 为 2×6，髋矩让腿角与机身俯仰可分别调节。）

**L2. Why is the model linear, and when does it break?**
> I linearize around the upright equilibrium with small angles, so sin θ ≈ θ. It breaks at large tilt, when the wheels leave the ground, and when the wheel torque saturates. In those cases I rely on the contact logic and the torque limits, not on the linear model.
（中文：小角度线性化；大倾角、离地、饱和时线性模型失效，靠接触逻辑和限幅兜底。）

**L2. Discrete or continuous design?**
> I design in continuous time and run at 500 Hz. Since 500 Hz is far above the closed-loop bandwidth of a few hertz, the difference from a discrete design is small. If the period were close to the dynamics I would use the discrete Riccati equation.
（中文：连续设计、500 Hz 运行；采样远高于带宽，差别小。）

**L3. LQR guarantees a phase margin of 60 degrees and infinite gain margin. Does that hold for you?**
> Only for full-state feedback, with exact states, a continuous plant and a diagonal R. In my robot the states are estimated, there is a 2 ms sampling delay, and the torque saturates, so those margins are not guaranteed. That is why I keep a safety margin by not pushing the weights up too far, and why I tune on the robot.
（中文：这些裕度只对精确全状态、连续、对角 R 成立；本项目有估计误差、延迟、饱和，所以不保证，要留余量并实车整定。）

**L3. What happens to LQR when the wheel torque saturates?**
> The gain is no longer effective: the real input is smaller than the commanded one, so the loop gain drops and the unstable mode can win. In my case the wheel torque limit is 3 N·m and a 0.5 m/s step would ask for about 9 N·m, so the robot fell. I fixed it with a one-second ramp on the command, an acceleration limit of 0.5 m/s², and a smaller velocity weight, not with a stronger gain. Anti-windup also matters for the small integral term in the leg-length loop.
（中文：饱和使有效增益下降；3 N·m 限幅 vs 约 9 N·m 需求；用斜坡、限加速度、降低速度权重解决，而不是加大增益；腿长环的积分项需抗饱和。）

**L3. How do the closed-loop poles move when the leg gets shorter?**
> In the simplified model the unstable pole scales roughly like 1/√L, from about 15.5 rad/s at 0.3 m to 26.7 rad/s at 0.1 m. A shorter leg falls faster, so the same gain gives less margin. This is the reason I schedule the gain by leg length.
（中文：不稳定极点约 ∝1/√L，0.3 m→0.1 m 从约 15.5 增到 26.7 rad/s；腿越短倒得越快，需要调度。）

**L3. Is the scheduled controller stable for any leg-length trajectory?**
> No. Each frozen-length design is stable, but the leg length changes in time, and that is a linear parameter-varying system. Stability is only guaranteed if the length changes slowly compared to the closed-loop dynamics. So I limit the rate of the leg-length reference and clamp the length inside the fitted range [0.10, 0.30] m. A guarantee would need a common Lyapunov function or an LPV synthesis, which I did not do.
（中文：冻结腿长下稳定不等于时变下稳定；靠限制腿长变化率和 clamp；严格保证需公共 Lyapunov 函数或 LPV 综合，本项目未做。）

**L3. Why a cubic polynomial and 48 coefficients?**
> K has 2×6 = 12 entries. Each entry is fitted as a cubic in the leg length, which has 4 coefficients, so 12 × 4 = 48. Cubic follows the smooth variation of the gains without the oscillation of high-order fits, and costs a few multiplications per cycle. I clamp the leg length so the polynomial is never evaluated outside the fitted range.
（中文：12 个增益×每个 4 个系数=48；三次足够平滑且不振荡，计算量小；在线 clamp。）

## 5. VMC 数学深挖（L1 / L2 / L3）

**L1. What is VMC in one sentence?**
> Virtual model control replaces the complicated closed-chain leg with a virtual leg, where I command a support force F along the leg and a torque Tp about the hip, and map them to the real joint motors with the Jacobian transpose.
（中文：VMC 用虚拟腿代替闭链腿，指令沿腿的支撑力 F 与髋部力矩 Tp，经雅可比转置映射到关节电机。）

**L2. Derive the torque map τ = Jᵀ F.**
> By virtual work, the power at the joints equals the power at the foot: τᵀq̇ = Fᵀẋ. Since ẋ = J q̇, τᵀq̇ = Fᵀ J q̇ for every q̇, so τ = JᵀF. For the virtual-leg coordinates (L₀, φ₀) the foot velocity is [L̇₀, L₀φ̇₀] = J_r J q̇, so τ = J_pᵀ [F; Tp] with J_p = J_r J. Here F is the force along the leg and Tp is the torque about the hip, and the conversion J_r uses the leg length.
（中文：虚功原理 τᵀq̇ = Fᵀẋ，得 τ=JᵀF；在虚拟腿坐标下 τ=J_pᵀ[F;Tp]，J_p=J_r J。）

**L2. How do you get the Jacobian of the five-bar?**
> The two distal links must meet at the foot point B, which gives two velocity constraints: u₁·Ḃ = L₁ sin(θ₁−α) α̇ and u₂·Ḃ = L₄ sin(θ₂−β) β̇. Stacking them gives M_c Ḃ = D_c q̇, so J = M_c⁻¹ D_c, and det M_c = sin(θ₂−θ₁).
（中文：两条远端杆在足端相交给出两个速度约束，叠起来得 J=M_c⁻¹D_c，det M_c=sin(θ₂−θ₁)。）

**L2. Give a numerical example (teaching example).**
> At α=135° and β=45° the Jacobian is [[−116.66, −116.66], [−97.24, 97.24]] mm/rad and the leg length is 298.1 mm. The rows of J_pᵀ in metres are [−0.0972, 0.3913] and [0.0972, 0.3913]. So a support force of 40 N gives joint torques of about ∓3.89 N·m, which is inside the 6 N·m joint limit but already above the rated torque of the motor. That is why I watch the force limit.
（中文：数值例；40 N 支撑力对应约 ∓3.89 N·m，在 6 N·m 软限幅内但已超过额定，因此要限力。教学示例。）

**L3. What happens near a singularity?**
> When the two distal links are collinear, sin(θ₂−θ₁) goes to zero, M_c becomes singular and the Jacobian blows up in one direction. The map from foot force to joint torque stays finite, but the map from joint speed to foot speed loses a direction, and small joint errors become large foot errors. I avoid it by keeping the leg length inside 0.10 to 0.30 m, away from the fully stretched pose, and by clamping the references.
（中文：远端杆共线时 det M_c→0，J 奇异；用腿长范围与参考限幅避开。）

**L3. Why does the transpose, not the inverse, appear?**
> Because forces and velocities are dual: the velocity map is J, and the power-conserving force map is its transpose. I never need to invert the Jacobian for torque control, so a singular M_c does not directly break the torque map, though large entries still mean large torques for small forces.
（中文：力与速度对偶，用转置保持功率；力矩映射不需要求逆。）

**L3. How do you stop the two legs from splitting or rolling?**
> I use a roll compensation that adds a differential force to the two legs with a proportional gain of about 80 N/rad and a damping term of about 6 N·s/rad, limited to ±10 N, and a coupling torque that keeps the two leg angles close. These are design values that I tuned on the robot.
（中文：横滚用左右差动力补偿 Kr≈80、Kω≈6、限幅 ±10 N；再加腿角耦合防劈叉。）

**L3. Where does the feedforward F = Mg/(2cosθ) come from?**
> Each leg carries half of the equivalent weight along the vertical, and the leg is tilted by θ, so the force along the leg is half of Mg divided by cos θ. With M ≈ 6 kg this is about 29.4 N per leg. The PID only handles the remaining error, so the integral term can stay small.
（中文：每腿承担一半重量，沿腿方向除以 cosθ；约 29.4 N/腿；PID 只处理剩余误差。）

## 5.1 工程化补充问答（J 组，L1 / L2 / L3）

**J1. (L1) At what rates does your system run?**
> The control and estimation loops run at 500 Hz, the IMU is sampled at 1 kHz with the latest sample taken each cycle, the FDCAN torque commands go out at 500 Hz, the remote command arrives at about 50 Hz, and the monitor runs at about 100 Hz.
（中文：控制与估计 500 Hz，IMU 1 kHz，FDCAN 命令 500 Hz，遥控约 50 Hz，监控约 100 Hz。）

**J2. (L2) What is your latency budget?**
> I split the loop into sensing, estimation, computation, bus transfer, motor current loop and zero-order hold. In our setup that is about 2.7 ms on average and about 5 ms in the worst case. At a 3 Hz crossover that costs about 5 degrees of phase, at 10 Hz about 18 degrees, so I kept the leg-length derivative gain modest. These are design budgets, not measured values. Details are in chapter 20.
（中文：拆成采样、估计、计算、总线、电机电流环、零阶保持；平均约 2.7 ms、最坏约 5 ms；3 Hz 约 5°，10 Hz 约 18°；均为设计预算而非实测，详见第 20 章。）

**J3. (L2) How did you prioritize FreeRTOS tasks?**
> The control task has the highest priority and is woken by a periodic tick, the IMU task is next, the CAN receive is an interrupt that only decodes and timestamps, and the communication and monitor tasks have the lowest priority. This keeps the jitter of the control task small.
（中文：控制任务最高优先级，IMU 次之，CAN 接收中断只解码打时间戳，通信与监控最低。）

**J4. (L3) How do you tune safely on the real robot?**
> I hang the robot first, check signs and zero offsets, then lower the torque limits and raise them step by step. I change one parameter at a time, log every run, keep a hand on the stop, and have a software cutoff for tilt beyond a threshold and for stale CAN data.
（中文：先吊起查符号和零位，降低力矩限幅再逐步放开，一次只改一个参数，记录日志，倾角过大或数据过期时软件切断输出。）

**J5. (L3) How did you go from simulation to the real robot?**
> I first checked the model and gains in simulation, then ran the real robot hung in the air to verify signs and the estimator, then with the wheels on the ground and low torque limits. The main differences were the pitch bias, friction, delay and torque limits, so I fixed the bias first and then tuned the weights.
（中文：先仿真，再吊起，再低力矩落地；主要差异为俯仰偏置、摩擦、延迟、限幅。）

**J6. (L2) How did you work with your supervisor and teammates?**
> My supervisor reviewed the plan and the results at regular meetings. A teammate owned the mechanical design and the motor wiring, so I agreed with them on sign conventions and the zero position before every test. I kept parameters in one versioned file so that any run could be reproduced.
（中文：导师定期评审；队友负责机械与接线，测试前约定符号与零位；参数集中版本管理，保证可复现。详见 [20 工程化实践](./20_工程化实践_频率周期与问题解决.md)。）

## 6. 为什么增益要按腿长调度（一分钟答法）

> Because A(L) and B(L) change. Mass matrix entries scale with L and L squared and the gravity term with L, so the leg-wheel unstable pole scales like 1/√L (15.5 to 26.7 for 0.3 to 0.1 m in my model) and the wheel-torque-to-leg-angle gain changes about fourfold. A gain designed at 0.2 m slows convergence by about 40% at 0.1 m and is unstable at 0.4 m in the model. So I compute K at several lengths and interpolate with polynomials.

（数字来自第 4、5 章教学模型，说"in a simplified model I used for design"。）
